how to read the stamps

every claim carries a stamp, so you can tell how much weight it bears. where the evidence is thin, conflicting or missing, we say so.

  • established established: stated in a standard, regulation, court order or controlled study.
  • corroborated corroborated: two or more independent sources agree.
  • single source single source: one study, agency statement or news report.
  • claim claim: asserted by an interested party, like a maker describing its own product, and not independently tested.
  • inference inference: our reasoning from the evidence.
  • not found not found: nothing reliable could be found.

the public edition

oct 2026draft

this is the public edition of the research brief, written by Crashfree India. we left out contact details and plans we haven’t finalised.

the short answer.

no product or machine on the market fixes India’s pothole problem on its own, and we couldn’t find one independent study in India that measured how long pothole repairs last. the few Indian field observations we found cover one to three patches, over one to six months.

what the evidence does support is a system: work out why each hole formed, fix the cause when it lies below the surface, make proper repairs in suitable weather, and measure what survives.

  • 2,385deaths police recorded as pothole-related in 2024 (about 1.3% of 1,77,175 road deaths)
  • 9,438over 2020–24
some states report no pothole deaths at all, so these numbers are a floor, not a full count. established
sources: MoRTH, Road Accidents in India 2024, Table 3.5 · Lok Sabha reply, 12 Feb 2026, via ThePrint

1. what counts as a pothole?

the definitions are close everywhere. what differs is the trigger: how deep a hole has to be before anyone has to act, and how fast.

India’s maintenance code (IRC:82-2015) describes a pothole as a bowl-shaped cavity in the bituminous surface, sometimes reaching into the layers below. it sizes them: small is up to 25 mm deep and 200 mm wide, medium is 25–50 mm deep and 500 mm wide, large is over 50 mm deep.

the same 40 mm-deep hole counts as moderate in US and NHAI schemes, triggers action in most UK councils, and wouldn’t breach Ontario’s standard until it reached 8 cm. so any comparison of pothole counts across cities or countries has to check which definition was used. established

on paper, India’s highway contract standard is among the strictest: a 10 mm limit and repair within 48 hours. we couldn’t find any record of how often it is enforced. not found

2. how a pothole forms.

a pothole is the last step in a chain of damage. the sources agree on the sequence. corroborated

  1. water gets in.the surface is meant to be a waterproof lid. cracks, open joints or a mix with too many air pockets let water through. once the air pockets pass about 8%, they tend to link up into channels (Pavement Interactive).
  2. the bitumen lets go.water creeps between the bitumen and the stone and peels it away, and softens the binder that holds the mix together.
  3. traffic pumps it.each passing tyre pushes water in and out of the surface, scouring the binder and washing out the fine material (Kringos 2007, TU Delft).
  4. stones fly out.stones come loose and tyres throw them out. the hole now holds more water, which speeds up the damage.
  5. the base gives way.if water reaches the stone base and the soil beneath, they lose strength. each wheel pushes muddy water up through the cracks, and the surface loses its support.
monsoon rain, waterlogged streets and heavy trucks combine exactly the conditions this needs. inference one Indian engineer’s plain-language version of the same physics ends: “drainage, drainage, drainage” (Shirish Patel, Scroll, 2024).
what makes it worseclose it
  • overloaded vehicles and thin pavements. a road too weak for its traffic bends and cracks into a chicken-wire pattern.
  • poor drainage, flat camber, ponding. water stays on or beside the road and soaks in.
  • a weak or saturated base. the stone layers and soil lose strength when wet.
  • poor construction. too little bitumen, poor compaction, a segregated mix, weak joints.
  • utility trenches refilled badly. loose refill keeps settling under traffic, so the surface sinks and cracks at the edges.
  • ageing. sun and air make bitumen stiff and brittle. freeze–thaw matters in India only at altitude.
you may have read that “70–80% of potholes form during the monsoon”. that claim traces back only to an unsourced website, so we didn’t use it. and no study measures how much of India’s pothole damage is down to the monsoon, overloading or poor construction. not found

3. surface or structural?

the Indian Roads Congress separates two kinds. non-structural potholes stay in the top layers. structural ones involve the base, sub-base and subgrade, and for those, treating the top layer “doesn’t last long” (Indian Highways, Dec 2017). the fix is to remove the saturated layers and rebuild.

a patch is a plug in the lid. if what lies beneath is wet and soft, the plug sits on nothing, the edges crack and the hole comes back. a South African guide is blunter: surface repair without fixing the cause is “normally a complete waste of time” (CSIR South Africa, 2010).

probably a surface problem. a good patch can work

  • an isolated hole in otherwise sound road
  • the road sheds water, no ponding nearby
  • the first failure at that spot
  • shallow, within the asphalt layers

probably structural or drainage. a patch only buys time

  • surrounded by interconnected “alligator” cracks, ruts or sagging
  • standing water, blocked drains, or mud pumping up through cracks
  • repeated failure at the same spot, along a utility trench or near a leaking pipe
  • deep, exposing a soft or muddy base
how often Indian potholes are structural is unknown. no dataset in India or abroad measures it. not found

can a pothole turn into a sinkhole? not directly. potholes form from the top down and are rarely deeper than 150–200 mm. a road cave-in forms from below: a leaking water main or broken sewer washes soil away and a void grows under an intact-looking surface. a hole that keeps sinking, or a dip over a pipeline, should be referred to the road owner and the utility, not patched.

4. how much of India is asphalt?

there is no recent official split of India’s road length by surface type that we could access. so we built an estimate. the network was about 63.31 lakh km in March 2019, about 65% of it surfaced (Factly, from MoRTH Basic Road Statistics).

  • 40–55%bituminous
  • 2–5%concrete
  • 10–20%water-bound macadam
  • ~35%unsurfaced
counting only asphalt and concrete, asphalt is roughly 90–96% of paved length. about 95% of India’s road length is non-concrete, and can develop potholes. concrete is common only in a few cities and parts of the national highway network. inference

5. how potholes get fixed.

about fifteen repair approaches are in use worldwide. they differ mostly in how the bitumen is made workable, and how it then hardens. Indian codes still treat hot mix as the preferred patching material, and cold mixes as the fallback where cold weather or heavy rain rule it out (IRC:SP:100-2014).

how each material hardensclose it
  • hot mix. bitumen heated and laid at about 135–148 °C. cools within minutes. the edges cool first, which weakens the bond and compaction there.
  • mastic asphalt. a binder-rich mix poured hot. it levels itself, so it is floated, not rolled.
  • cutback cold mix. bitumen thinned with a solvent (kerosene in the Indian code). the solvent evaporates over days to weeks. fire hazard: no open flame.
  • emulsion cold mix. tiny bitumen droplets in water. it hardens as the emulsion “breaks” and the water leaves, over hours to weeks, slower in cold, damp or humid air.
  • reactive (“instant”) mix. the binder reacts with water, or contains cement. opened to traffic in 3–35 minutes in a Japanese trial. full cure took about 3–5 days in a 2025 study.
  • cement-type (rapid-set concrete, geopolymer). sets in 15–60 minutes. much stiffer than asphalt, so the joint can crack.
two points. every material has a hardening phase: cold mixes just slow down most in cold, damp weather. and “open to traffic in 10 minutes” is not the same as “fully cured”. some products carry traffic within minutes, but full strength takes days (Otaru Road Office; Xu et al. 2025).
the methods, comparedclose it

sources: FHWA-RD-98-073, FHWA-RD-99-168, IRC:116, MnDOT 2018, Ohio DOT 2014.

  • throw-and-goestablished

    mix shovelled into an uncleaned hole and left for traffic to compact. lasts “a few hours to a few weeks”, and is the dearest over its life once re-patching is counted. not a repair: use it only to make a hazard safe.

  • throw-and-rollestablished (US)

    the same, then the truck drives over the patch. with good material it matched the careful method in a large US study. with a poor mix, or in winter, it did much worse.

  • prepared cold-mix patchstandard established life: not found

    clean the hole, remove the water, tack coat, layers up to 75 mm, compact the edges first. a damp hole is allowed under the Indian code.

  • hot-mix cut-and-patchcorroborated

    saw-cut to sound road, clean, rebuild the lower layers, tack, place hot mix in layers, roll, seal the joint. needs a dry hole and air above 10 °C. the best-supported permanent repair, and the lowest cost per year of life in one Canadian comparison.

  • edge or joint sealmixed

    a band of binder and sand over the joint. needs a dry surface. mostly no significant benefit in the US study.

  • spray injection (jet patching)established abroad claim in india

    an air blast clears water and debris, emulsion is sprayed as tack, coated stone is blown in at speed, no roller. as good as control patches at all sites in the US study, but it depends on the operator, and it isn’t suited to deep holes.

  • automated truck patchersclaim

    one vehicle cuts, cleans, tacks, places and compacts hot mix, logging GPS and photos. no independent durability data. one Canadian city withdrew its unit after it was out of service for 127 days in its first nine months.

  • machine-mixed cold mixclaim

    a truck-mounted unit mixes emulsion cold mix on site. we found no independent field test.

  • infrared pre-heatingmixed

    a heater softens the old asphalt around the hole so it can be reworked with new mix. 14 of 15 patches survived 188 days in one Ohio winter trial, but 18 of the first 26 at the main site of a Texas heat trial were badly damaged. no Indian deployment found.

  • mastic asphaltclaim

    hot, binder-rich mix poured into a cut hole. used at city scale in at least one very wet metro. no independent survival data.

  • engineered proprietary cold mixestested products only

    bagged or bulk mixes with modified binders. in Virginia, all 13 products beat the state’s standard mix, which failed within 14 days. how they do in Indian conditions: unknown.

  • reactive / water-activated mixesclaim

    the binder cures on contact with water. fast reopening shown, full cure takes days, no long-term field data.

  • rapid-set cementitiousclaim

    cement-type fill setting in minutes to an hour. stiffer than asphalt, so joints crack. no patch survival data. suits concrete roads, not general asphalt potholes.

  • steel-slag instant mixclaim

    processed steel slag with a polymer binder, poured from bags. claimed to work without drying the hole or a tack coat. no published lab or field results. steel slag can contain free lime that swells with water.

  • full-depth repaircode-based

    remove the failed layers to firm ground, fix the drainage, rebuild in compacted layers. the only durable option where the base or soil has failed.

  • preventive treatmentsindirect

    crack sealing, slurry, surface dressing: seal the surface before water gets in. extends pavement life by about 2–3 years. no study directly counts the potholes prevented.

five things decide whether a patch lasts

  1. preparation.remove loose material and water. cut back to sound road with vertical edges. clean with compressed air. water and debris at the bottom stop the patch bonding.
  2. bond at the edge.tack coat on the base and the sides. heat or seal the joint. the joint is “the area that fails most frequently”.
  3. material.a mix suited to the conditions, tested with the local stone, and no non-bituminous fill. cheap local cold mix did significantly worse at three of eight US sites.
  4. compaction.compact the edges first, in layers. a roller, not a hand tamper, for hot mix. poor compaction causes dips, shoving and ravelling.
  5. support and drainage.diagnose first. repair the base and drains where they caused the hole. otherwise the patch only treats the symptom.

and the weather decides a lot

in the largest controlled study (about 1,250 patches, US and Canada), site conditions and material quality mattered more than the method’s name. patches at dry sites survived 85% of the time. at wet sites, 48%. corroborated the effect of monsoon rain itself: not found

more conditionsclose it
  • cold. at 0 °C all tested materials were very weak. patches placed in snowy months lasted 10–25 days, against 60–100 days in other months. India’s road specification bars bituminous laying below 10 °C. corroborated
  • heat. we found no field study above 40 °C air temperature. not found
  • heavy traffic. the busiest US site lost most of its patches by 133 weeks. single source
  • tropical monsoon. no rigorous field study found. not found
  • hills and steep grades. no study of patch life on slopes found. not found
  • deep holes. fill holes deeper than 50 mm in two layers. corroborated
  • weak base, poor drainage. identical patches at one site survived anywhere from 30% to 80%, depending on support and drainage. established
three questions people askclose it

is quick “throw-and-roll” really as good as a careful repair? sometimes. in the large US study it “proved just as effective” as the semi-permanent method for the materials compared directly, and was about five times faster. it matches the careful method only with high-quality material and real compaction. the careful method is more forgiving of everything else.

how do humid countries make sure the hole is dry? mostly they wait for dry weather, add crews and use temporary fills in between. Singapore’s transport authority told its Parliament that patching materials only bond properly on a dry surface. drying tools exist (compressed air, hot-air lances, the air blast of jet patchers, infrared heaters), but no study measures how much they extend patch life in the tropics.

does compaction matter for every method? for hot mix it is critical, and only possible while the mix is hot. for cold mix it matters because loose patches let water in and sink. spray injection compacts by the force of placement. mastic is poured and floated, not rolled.

6. does anyone know how long a repair lasts?

we searched Indian journals, institutional repositories, court records, government-lab publications and the reporting on each city programme. we found no independent, systematic field study in India of how long pothole repairs survive. not found

the few field observations that exist are very small and short. the best documented checked three patches on a busy Delhi highway: after six months of heavy rain the material hadn’t come out (Gola et al. 2018). there is a student project on three potholes over one month. there is an expert-opinion survey of 23 experts, which is not field measurement. and there is a government-funded project (2023–26) with planned field validation, whose field results aren’t out yet (SRM University-AP project page).

a 2026 review of cold-mix repair by Indian researchers also cites no Indian field study of patch performance (Manjushree & Lokeshwari 2026). claims of “long-lasting” repairs from developers and agencies come with no published data. claim

the repair figures we found count potholes filled. we couldn’t find any that counted how many stayed filled. the gap in evidence is itself a central finding. not found

7. the ideal system.

no single method or product fits every Indian condition, least of all the monsoon. the ideal is a system in which each method does the job it is best at. inference

  1. prevent.seal cracks, clear drains and resurface on schedule. the Indian Roads Congress calls this “a stitch in time”.
  2. triage.classify each pothole as surface or structural. flag trenches, ponding and repeat failures.
  3. make safe.in wet weather, use a tested interim material, logged as temporary and scheduled for permanent repair.
  4. repair permanently.in a dry window, to the national specification: cut to sound road, tack, compact, seal the joint.
  5. fix the cause.full-depth repair, drainage and utility reinstatement by the road owner.
  6. verify and pay for survival.independent sample inspections. guarantees and deductions for repeat failure.
  7. certify materials.an Indian “monsoon patch” approval with a monitored road trial. the UK requires a two-year one (HAPAS SG8).
the missing piece is a proven wet-season material. no product has independent survival data under monsoon conditions. that is the central research question. inference

what a group like ours can do now: triage, making safe, permanent repair in dry windows, and independent verification, all with the road owner’s permission. prevention and structural repair need the owner’s budget and engineers.

8. how a fair test works.

a pilot that produces the missing evidence would be valuable in its own right. here is what a fair test needs.

  1. matched arms.three or four options at comparable sites (same road class, traffic and pothole sizes), plus the road owner’s current practice as a control. randomise which method goes where, if possible.
  2. triage first.for each pothole, record depth, size, surrounding cracking, ponding, trenches and repeat failure. analyse structural and waterlogged spots separately, because no patch can be judged fairly there.
  3. define failure in advance.inspect at fixed intervals (for example 1, 3, 6, 9 and 12 months), through at least one monsoon.
  4. measure what matters.survival rate, time to make safe, cost per durable repair including repeat visits, and incidents at the sites. geo-tagged photos prove time and place, not quality, so add random independent checks.
  5. an independent evaluator.someone with no commercial stake. makers shouldn’t choose the sites or inspect their own patches.
  6. enough patches.the big US study used about 1,250. one Ohio trial’s 15 per method was too few to generalise. aim for dozens per method per city.

and only the road owner can authorise work on its roads. we found no published route for NGOs, so a written MoU or work order is the realistic one. work on most public roads is judged against the national specification (MoRTH Specifications cl. 3004.2): vertical edges, tack coat, compaction, no non-bituminous fill.

what we ask before we trust a productclose it

“works in water” and “ready in minutes” claims are common. none of the products we found has public, independent Indian survival data. before any product goes into a test, we ask for:

  1. the binder type and curing mechanism (emulsion, cutback, polymer-reactive, cementitious, slag-based) and the safety data sheet.
  2. time to traffic and time to full cure at about 5 °C and 35 °C, and in a wet hole.
  3. independent test reports, not the maker’s own: coating and stripping, bond to old asphalt, rutting at 60 °C and, for slag products, volume stability.
  4. field survival data: how many patches, where, the climate, the traffic, the follow-up period, the definition of failure and who inspected.
  5. any accreditation or approval, and the standard it was tested against.
  6. a written guarantee with re-repair at the maker’s cost, and agreement to independent inspection.
  7. for cutback-based mixes, fire and storage precautions.

9. what we looked for and couldn’t find.

the gaps are part of the result. these are things we searched for and came back empty.

  • how long a patch lasts in tropical monsoon conditions, on slopes, or above 40 °C.
  • independent tests of any Indian “instant” or steel-slag mix.
  • how often Indian potholes are structural. no dataset in India or abroad measures it.
  • how much of India’s pothole damage comes from the monsoon, from overloading or from poor construction.
  • an official split of India’s roads by surface type. our section above is an estimate.
  • a pothole death count that isn’t a floor. some states report none at all.
  • the current edition of India’s maintenance code (IRC:82-2023), and the list of accredited pothole products. we couldn’t get either.

the words.

open the glossaryclose it
aggregate
crushed stone, gravel and sand: the part of asphalt that carries the load.
bitumen
the black, sticky crude-oil product that binds stones together (“asphalt binder” in North America).
cutback
bitumen thinned with a solvent such as kerosene so it can be used cold. hardens as the solvent evaporates.
emulsion
tiny bitumen droplets suspended in water. hardens when the droplets merge and the water leaves.
hot mix
stone and bitumen mixed and laid hot (about 135–148 °C). hardens on cooling.
mastic asphalt
a binder-rich hot mix that is poured and smoothed, not rolled.
base, sub-base, subgrade
the stone layers and natural soil beneath the asphalt surface.
stripping
water separating bitumen from stone.
ravelling
stones coming loose and being lost from the surface.
alligator cracking
interconnected cracks like crocodile skin, usually a sign of structural weakness.
pumping
water and fine material forced up through cracks by passing wheels.
tack coat / prime coat
a thin spray of binder that helps new material stick to old asphalt (tack) or seals a stone base (prime).
debonding
a patch coming loose from the road around it.
throw-and-go / throw-and-roll
shovelled repairs, either left alone or compacted by the truck’s tyres.
spray injection (jet patching)
a machine blows the hole clean and sprays in binder-coated stone.
full-depth repair
removing and rebuilding all failed layers.
